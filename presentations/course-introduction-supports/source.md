## Purpose and use

This is the shared, self-contained editorial source for an introduction shown at the beginning of any Cyberjutsu course. It supplies the content, narrative, participant guidance, vocabulary, visual direction, and source provenance needed to generate a presentation, video, or infographic. It replaces the former practitioner-foundations note and retains the foundations relevant to a course introduction.

All audience-facing text and narration must be in English. Retain proper names such as WOCSA and Cité des sciences et de l'industrie. Pair customary Japanese writing with rōmaji and a short English meaning. Cyberjutsu is also written Cyberjūtsu, サイバー術 (Saibā-jutsu); retain the established Cyberjutsu brand spelling in headlines and URLs.

Address beginners and experienced practitioners together. The intended progression is: understand WOCSA, feel invited to contribute, discover Cyberjutsu, understand how to practise together, and become ready to begin. Use a welcoming, practical, confident tone. This is a reusable course introduction, with historical examples clearly identified as such.

## 1. WOCSA: who we are, what we do, and how we work

WOCSA means **Worldwide Open Cyber Security Association**. It is a federation of nonprofit associations rooted in France. It connects experts and people discovering cybersecurity, combining international projects with local action. Cybersecurity concerns everyone, and everyone can contribute to improving digital life. These ideas recur in the 2024 and 2026 WOCSA decks.

The decks' English sharing message is:

> In Cybersecurity too, knowledge only increases in value once shared.

WOCSA promotes education, awareness, and practical solutions through open collaboration. Its website identifies five values: collaboration, transparency, innovation, inclusion, and hands-on learning. Explain these as working together, sharing knowledge, trying ideas, welcoming different backgrounds, and learning through practice. Source: [WOCSA](https://wocsa.org/).

### Organisation

Country associations have their own legal entities and finances, with affiliation and charter agreements connecting them to the French WOCSA association. Chapters have a leader and second leader; projects also have a leader and second leader and work across chapters. The website lists a Main chapter for global governance and country chapters for local communities. Sources: [WOCSA organisation](https://wocsa.org/), [chapters](https://wocsa.org/pages/chapters.php).

For a simple organisation diagram, show three connected levels: shared international coordination, country chapters, and local activities. Draw cross-chapter projects across that structure. The 2026 deck's French Office example adds a president, secretary and deputy, treasurer and deputy, and local zones. Present these as roles, without turning the dated slide's officeholders into a current directory.

## 3. Cyberjutsu: the digital martial art

### Definition and purpose

Cyberjutsu is an educational approach to cybersecurity inspired by modern Japanese martial arts, especially 柔道 (Jūdō, judo), 空手 (Karate, karate), and 柔術 (Jūjutsu, jujutsu). It lets novices and experts practise together through repeated exercises and controlled attack-and-defense exchanges.

It follows 武道 (Budō, the martial way): technical progress goes together with ethical conduct, discipline, judgment, and contribution to society. WOCSA began the project in 2020, according to the repository's principles document.

Use the poster's main message translated into English: **“Cyberjutsu: the digital martial art.”** Its explanatory promise becomes: **“Japanese martial arts applied to cybersecurity. Learn digital combat as you would train on the tatami.”** Its accessible invitation becomes: **“Open to everyone — no expertise required.”** The poster's age range belongs to its specific event; general course admission conditions remain with the organiser.

The aim is the most efficient use of body, mind, and computer, with mutual benefit from human confrontation. Training should help people live better in cyberspace through technical growth, attention, self-control, and responsibility.

Sources: 2024 deck, slides 6–8 of its DEF CON edition; 2026 deck, slides 8–10; 2026 poster; [repository introduction](../../README.md); [principles](../../principle.md).

### Why apply martial arts to cybersecurity?

The five reasons in the WOCSA decks are:

1. Improve coordination between mind and movement.
2. Train to act under pressure.
3. Develop fast and accurate reflexes.
4. Learn to improvise in unfamiliar situations.
5. Learn a code of conduct and respect for others.

Explain the connection with one simple example: a practitioner notices a connection, understands what is happening, chooses an appropriate response, and learns from the partner's reaction. Repetition makes that sequence more familiar; freer practice develops adaptation. The partner is essential to learning.

### Two guiding principles

- **精力善用 (Seiryoku zen'yō)** — make the best use of energy: seek the simplest, most effective action for the situation. The repository uses “maximum efficiency with minimum effort” and a different Japanese rendering, 精力最活用; use the conventional judo term consistently in these supports.
- **自他共栄 (Jita kyōei)** — mutual welfare and benefit: create conditions in which both partners improve.

Connect them explicitly: an efficient action is valuable when it contributes to disciplined learning and mutual progress.

Terminology references: [Kodokan explanation of Seiryoku-Zenyo](https://kdkjudo.org/%E7%B2%BE%E5%8A%9B%E5%96%84%E7%94%A8%E3%81%A8%E3%81%AF/), [International Judo Federation on mutual aid](https://www.ijf.org/history/judo-culture/2290).

### What practice looks like

The **道場 (Dōjō)** is the place of practice, including the isolated digital training environment. A **先生 (Sensei)** guides the session. A Cyberjutsuka is a Cyberjutsu practitioner. A cyberdeck is the practitioner's training computer, prepared to give partners equal starting conditions.

In paired practice, **取り (Tori)** performs the technique being studied and **受け (Uke)** receives it. These are learning roles: Tori may perform a defensive technique while Uke supplies an incoming connection. Partners exchange roles.

A typical session moves through greetings, warmup, demonstration, repeated exercises, agreed practice, freer sparring, and reflection. Introduce only the vocabulary needed for that journey:

| Japanese and rōmaji | Meaning in Cyberjutsu |
| --- | --- |
| 礼 (Rei), 礼儀 (Reigi) | Bow, respect, and etiquette. |
| 打ち込み (Uchikomi) | Repeat an entry or technical pattern to improve precision. |
| 投げ込み (Nagekomi) | Repeat the technique through its completion. |
| 型 (Kata) | A repeatable form that preserves and teaches a principle. |
| 約束稽古 (Yakusoku geiko) | Agreed practice with defined roles and constraints. |
| 乱取り (Randori) | Freer practice with a partner within the dojo rules. |
| 黙想 (Mokusō) | Quiet reflection on the experience. |

Computer-based practice includes reading activity, connecting to a partner, and responding to a connection. It is guided practical training. Keep detailed command lines and exercise timings in the course material, so this introduction works with different sessions.

## 4. Enjoy the session and help your partner progress

### Cyberjutsuka Goal

Keep this heading. The following is an English copy edit of the content on slide 9 of the 2024 DEF CON deck:

- Treat Cyberjutsu as a continuing educational practice: improve through attack and defense.
- Develop yourself and contribute to society.
- Make the best use of energy and seek mutual prosperity.
- Think about your partner as well as yourself; put each other in the best conditions to progress together.
- Develop a way to live your digital life that goes beyond the technical aspects.

The practical message is personal progress and shared learning. Avoid presenting an unsourced historical quotation as something said by a named martial arts teacher; express the ideas in the narrator's own voice.

### Hands-on Cyberjutsu rules to ensure success of the human experience

Keep this heading. Preserve all four requirements from slide 10 of the 2024 DEF CON deck, with corrected English:

1. **Use only the techniques and commands taught during the workshop.**
2. **Participate in all workshop activities.** The workshop is a complete learning sequence, not an à-la-carte selection.
3. **Practise with a mutual-aid mindset.** We are here to become better together.
4. **Follow the teacher's instructions carefully.**

These rules are textual content from the deck. Their visual treatment must follow the 2026 poster, as requested. The poster itself does not contain these two sections.

### Supporting dojo etiquette

The repository's rules and moral code add the following practical guidance:

- Respect the dojo, teacher, partner, and yourself. Greet your partner before practice and thank them afterward.
- Listen to the Sensei, wait for instructions before using the cyberdeck, and ask before entering or leaving during training.
- Practise only inside the closed dojo network, using the authorised cyberdecks of the partners engaged in the exercise.
- Begin with equal equipment and configuration. Keep the required services available and use only the allowed practice credentials.
- Allow a real exchange: sustained blocking, purely defensive avoidance, and only pretending to attack prevent shared practice.
- Show courage, sincerity, etiquette, honour, humility, self-control, mutual aid, perseverance, integrity, and kindness.

For enjoyment and progress, use mistakes as feedback, ask for help, repeat patiently, exchange roles, and help your partner succeed. These are facilitation recommendations derived from mutual aid and the course structure.

Explain the session signals before the start:

| Signal | What the practitioner does |
| --- | --- |
| 始め (Hajime) — begin | Begin or resume on the instructor's signal. |
| 待て (Mate) — stop | Stop actions and return to the starting state, with a clear prompt and no running actions remaining. |
| そのまま (Sono mama) — hold the position | Preserve the current situation without performing new actions. |
| それまで (Sore made) — end | End the exchange and stop actions. |

Sources: [fight rules](../../rules.md), [moral code](../../moral-code.md), [glossary](../../glossary.md), [introductory course](../../courses/esgi-2026/short-introduction-cyberjutsu.md). Keep normal kana where appropriate; do not invent kanji to decorate a command.

## Practitioner foundations for supporting explanations

These ideas preserve the useful background from the previous foundations note. Use them for presenter explanations or a longer introduction; the short opening need not teach every concept.

A Cyberjutsu technique, 技 (Waza), is a short command or command sequence that can be learned, memorised, spelled aloud, and typed by hand. Linux is the main reference environment. A technique should be repeatable, useful, efficient in human effort, and meaningful within an attack, defense, perception, or control principle. Mappings to MITRE ATT&CK, D3FEND, or CAPEC can help explain technical purpose, but do not replace the educational and martial principles.

The main families include 読み技 (Yomi waza), reading and interpreting activity; 攻撃技 (Kōgeki waza), attack; and 防御技 (Bōgyo waza), defense. Perception precedes action: observe, identify, interpret, act, reset, and repeat. A practitioner learns to choose a useful action at the right time, not simply collect commands.

作り (Tsukuri) means preparing the conditions for action; 崩し (Kuzushi) means disrupting balance or structure; 掛け (Kake) means executing the technique. In a digital exercise, these may involve arranging monitoring, disrupting the partner's expected line of action, and applying the appropriate response.

Initiative can be studied through 後の先 (Go no sen), responding after an action begins; 先の先 (Sen no sen), acting at its inception; and 先々の先 (Sen sen no sen), anticipating it. 守破離 (Shuhari) expresses progression from learning the form to exploring variation and eventually acting with understanding and freedom.

The repository's kata explore perception, reading, initiative, reversal, balance breaking, preparation, and deception. Kata preserve principles for study; randori tests their use in less scripted exchanges. Warmup prepares eyes, hands, attention, and typing. Repetition and role exchange develop precision. Reflection helps turn an exercise into lasting judgment and habits.
